What you're looking at

The copper trace is a transmission line with a characteristic impedance of 50 ohms. A wave leaves the connector on the left and travels toward the load on the right. If the load is also 50 ohms, the line can't tell where it ends: all the power goes into the load and the wave arrives with nothing to send back. The shaded envelope is flat.

Any other load reflects part of the wave. The reflected wave runs back toward the source and adds to the next incoming one. Where they line up the voltage swings wider, and where they oppose it nearly cancels, and those points stay in place: a standing wave. The envelope's ripple is the mismatch made visible. The ratio of its peak to its dip is the VSWR.

Reading the Smith chart

The chart maps every possible load onto a disc. The center is a perfect match. The edge is total reflection: a short on the left, an open on the right. Upper half is inductive, lower half capacitive. The distance from the center is how much comes back, and the dashed circle through the point is every load with that same VSWR. A matching network's whole job is to walk a load from wherever it lands to the center.

Why line length matters

Changing the length is the same as changing frequency: what counts is how many wavelengths fit between source and load. Slide it and the pattern shifts along the trace, but the ripple keeps its size. That's why a match at one frequency can be a poor one at another, and why the amplifier was measured across its whole band, not at one point.

What's simplified

  • The line is lossless and the source is matched, so there are no re-reflections from the left end.
  • The load is a fixed impedance. A real component changes with frequency, which is most of what makes matching hard.
  • Heights are voltage, not distance. The wave is drawn above the trace; the actual fields live in the substrate between the trace and the ground plane underneath.
